Two systems can each be correct — and still disagree.

Reconciliation breaks are rarely caused by a bug. They're caused by exactly that.

Here's the trace:

A customer pays $10,000 by card. The moment the gateway's webhook says “success,” your ledger marks the transaction captured at $10,000 — the full amount the customer was charged. Nothing failed. Your ledger is correct.

Two days later, the gateway's settlement batch actually credits your bank account. But it doesn't credit $10,000 — it credits $9,800, after deducting its ~2% MDR fee up front. The gateway's settlement file is also correct; it was always going to net the fee before payout, that's contractually how it works.

Neither system is wrong. But your ledger says $10,000 and your bank account says $9,800, and unless your reconciliation logic explicitly accounts for the fee, that $200 gap looks unexplained. Now multiply this by partial captures, refunds issued from the gateway's dashboard your system never got a webhook for, FX conversions the gateway snapshots at its own rate and timestamp, and a settlement batch that runs T+2 instead of same-day — and you get a drift that compounds daily and doesn't announce itself as an error anywhere.

What a real EOD recon job needs to catch this:
→

Three-way matching, not two. Comparing your ledger against the gateway's report isn't enough — you also need the actual bank statement.

→

Match on a stable identifier, not amount-and-date heuristics — the gateway's transaction reference, the same id your idempotency key should already be tracking.

→

A rolling window, not a same-day cutoff. Settlement lag (T+1, T+2) means a transaction legitimately won't match today and will match cleanly tomorrow.

→

Unresolved breaks post to a suspense account, not just an alert someone dismisses — keeping the books balanced while it's investigated.

Recon isn't a batch job you write once. The day you add a new payment method, a new PSP, or change a fee structure, an assumption baked into your matching logic quietly stops holding — and the drift comes back in a new shape.
